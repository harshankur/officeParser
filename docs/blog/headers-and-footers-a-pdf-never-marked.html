Headers and Footers a PDF Never Marked

A Word document's header is a part of its own, with a page number field in it. A PDF's is text drawn again on every page. Getting the header back means comparing the pages, and knowing when not to.

HA
Harsh Ankur
Published 29 October 2026 · 9 min read

Convert a forty-page report from PDF to text and count how often you read its "Page N of 40" line and the report's title. In a Word document that is never a question: the header is a part of its own, written once, and the page number in it is a field that Word fills in on each page. A PDF has no such part. Its header is just text drawn near the top of every page, forty times over.

Until 8.4.1 officeParser read it that way, as body text, on every page. This post is about how 8.4.1 finds a PDF's running headers and footers when nothing marks them, how it turns a printed "3" back into a page number field, and why it leaves some headers where they are.

When the PDF Says So

A tagged PDF does mark them. Its text is in a structure tree of headings, paragraphs and tables, and content outside the structure is wrapped as an artifact: 

/Artifact << /Type /Pagination /Subtype /Footer >> BDC
  BT /F1 9 Tf 175 30 Td (Page 3 of 4) Tj ET
EMC

officeParser already read a tagged PDF's artifacts in the top and bottom 15% of a page into auxiliary.headers and auxiliary.footers, where it puts a Word document's header. Many PDFs are not tagged, though. One from an older word processor or a reporting tool is often just text at positions. Here is a small one I made with pdf-lib: four pages of garden notes, each with a header, a heading, a line of text and a footer. officeParser 8.4.0 read it like this: 

Field Notes, Spring 2026
Planting
The beds went in on the first dry week of March.

                            Page 1 of 4

Field Notes, Spring 2026
Watering
Rain did most of the work until the middle of April.

                             Page 2 of 4

Comparing the Pages

Nothing on one page marks its header, so 8.4.1 looks across the pages. It takes each line in a page's top or bottom 15% and compares it with the lines in the same band on the other pages. Two lines match when they sit the same distance from the page's edge (within 2 points), start at the same left edge (within 4), and say the same thing. A line is a running line when it is on at least two in five of the pages read, and on three of them at least.

"Say the same thing" is the hard part, because a footer's whole point is that it changes: "Page 1 of 4", "Page 2 of 4". So before lines are compared, the numbers in them that are the page's own are set aside, and "Page 1 of 4" on page 1 and "Page 2 of 4" on page 2 become the same line. A number is set aside when it counts up one a page ("3" on page 3 and "4" on page 4, or "149" and "150" in an article printed from 147) and it is set apart as a page number is: alone on the line ("3", "- 3 -"), after a word like "Page", as "3 of 4", or at the end after a bar ("Annual Report | 3"). A number after any other word names something of its own. Only a number that is the page's own, a "3" on page 3, later becomes a page field. A line that is the page's number and nothing more ("3", "- 3 -", "Page 3 of 4") counts on two pages, so a two-page letter's numbers are found.

Then there are the lines that look like headers and are not. Each of these stays in the page's text:

  • A number that names something else. "Question 3" at the top of page 3 and "Question 4" at the top of page 4 are two questions, not one header, even though their numbers match the pages'.
  • A date, or a serial or ticket number, which changes in ways no page count explains.
  • A heading. A line larger than the body's text is the page's, and so is everything between it and the page's text, even when it is repeated on every page.
  • A table's repeated head row. It is repeated at the top of each page the table runs on, but it stands close to the rows under it, so it reads with them. A running header stands apart from the text below it by at least its own height.

On the garden notes, the header and the footer are each the same on all four pages once the numbers are blanked, and both are smaller than the text and stand well apart from it. They go to auxiliary, and the text is the notes alone: 

Planting
The beds went in on the first dry week of March.

Watering
Rain did most of the work until the middle of April.

A Printed "3" Becomes a Field

Taking the footer out of the text is half the job. A writer that lays out pages, such as Word, ODT, RTF or LaTeX, puts it back as a real footer, and the footer it writes has to number its own pages. If officeParser kept "Page 1 of 4" as text, every page of the Word file would say "Page 1 of 4". In 8.4.1 the numbers that matched the page become page fields in the AST. The page's own number is marked metadata.field: 'page' and the page count 'pages'. Each writer then writes the field its format has: 

<w:t>Page </w:t><w:fldSimple w:instr=" PAGE ">…1…</w:fldSimple>
<w:t> of </w:t><w:fldSimple w:instr=" NUMPAGES ">…4…</w:fldSimple>

That is the footer of ast.to('docx'), shortened. In LaTeX output the same footer is \thepage{} and \pageref*{LastPage}, with the lastpage package for the count. Before 8.4.1 the LaTeX writer printed the number that was saved, the same on every page, and did that for a Word or ODT document's page fields too. The page number keeps its style: where the PDF's page labels number its pages in roman numerals, the field is in roman numerals too.

There is one exception. When you read only part of a PDF, with a pageRange that starts after page 1, a field would number the output's first page 1 where the PDF printed 5. So the numbers are not made fields, and since the footer then reads differently on each page, it stays in each page's text as it was printed. A range from page 1 that stops early does get a page field, but its count of pages stays as printed, since a field would count only the pages read.

Headers That Stay in Their Pages

Finding running lines is per line. Moving them to auxiliary is a choice for the whole document, made for the header and the footer apart, and it has to answer to the writers. A writer that lays out pages prints a header from auxiliary on every page. So a header goes there only if it is the same on every page read, its page numbers aside. I ran five small documents of six pages each through 8.4.1 to show where the line falls:

The header isWhere it goes
The same on every pageauxiliary.headers
A chapter's head, "Chapter 1" on two pages, then "Chapter 2"Stays in each page's text
On some pages onlyStays in each page's text
On every page but the first, a coverauxiliary.headers
One on the cover, another on every later pageThe cover's stays on the cover, the other goes to auxiliary.headers

A chapter's head is a running line, but no single header could print it, so moving it would lose the second chapter's. The same goes for a header that only some pages have. A cover is the one case I allowed. The first page of three or more may have no header at all (in an untagged PDF the others must still be three, so the header is found at all). If it has a header of its own while every later page agrees on another, the cover's stays on the cover and the rest go to auxiliary. These rules for what stays in the pages apply to a tagged PDF's artifacts too. Before 8.4.1 a tagged PDF's chapter heads went to auxiliary whatever they said, so Word output printed the first chapter's head on every page and lost the rest.

The rule also puts a header that stays back where it was drawn: the header before the page's text, and the footer after it. And a header's line is a paragraph, never a heading, even where a tagged PDF set it larger than the text, so a document's outline does not fill up with its running titles.

Choosing for Yourself

Two settings change all of this: 

// Every page keeps its own header and footer, where they stand.
await parseOffice('notes.pdf', { pdfParserConfig: { keepHeadersAndFootersInPages: true } });

// No headers or footers at all.
await parseOffice('notes.pdf', { ignoreHeadersAndFooters: true });

The first is for output that shows each page as it was, such as a slide per page, or for when you want the running title in the text. The page numbers stay fields there too. The second drops every line found to repeat at the top or bottom of the pages, including the ones the default keeps in their pages, such as a chapter's running head.

What It Does Not Do Yet

A few limits are worth knowing. A book often prints one head on even pages and another on odd ones: the book's title on the left, the chapter's on the right. officeParser finds these lines, but it does not yet write even- and odd-page headers, so they stay in their pages. And when a cover has no header, the other pages' header goes to auxiliary, but officeParser's writers do not yet give a PDF's cover a first-page header of its own, so the cover gets that header too. Roman page numbers are read as page numbers only where the PDF's page labels say so: without labels, "i, ii, iii" stay in the text. Front matter numbered i to iii before a body numbered in digits has a footer that changes its style partway, so it stays in its pages as printed. And an article cut from a journal, its pages printed from 147, keeps its page numbers in its text, because they are not the pages' own numbers.

None of these loses text. That was the rule I held the whole feature to. When officeParser is not sure a line is a header that one header part could hold, it leaves the line on its page. A repeated title in your text is a nuisance, but a chapter heading that disappears is a bug.
