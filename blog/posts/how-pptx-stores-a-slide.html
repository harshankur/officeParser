Developers building document pipelines often treat PowerPoint (.pptx) as "Word with page breaks". That assumption is where broken text extraction, scrambled reading order, and lost speaker notes come from.

PresentationML describes a layout, not a flow. Text does not exist on its own as prose. It exists inside shapes that are placed on a canvas.

1. The Four-Tier Inheritance Tree

Open the XML of a slide, say ppt/slides/slide1.xml, and you will often find text with no font size, no font family, no bold flag, and no colour.

PowerPoint knows how to draw it because each slide sits at the bottom of a four-level chain: 

Theme (theme1.xml)
└── Slide Master (slideMaster1.xml)
    └── Slide Layout (slideLayout2.xml)
        └── Slide (slide1.xml)

A shape marked <p:ph type="title"/> is a placeholder. It takes its position and text formatting from the matching placeholder in its layout. The layout takes its defaults from the master. The master takes its colours (accent1, tx1) and fonts (majorFont, minorFont) from the theme.

What this means for a parser

A reader that looks only at slide1.xml cannot tell whether a title is 32pt bold or 14pt italic. To know how the text looks, it has to follow the relationships back through the layout, the master, and the theme.

2. The EMU: Why 914,400?

Open any shape in a slide's XML and the coordinates look absurdly large: 

<a:xfrm>
  <a:off x="1524000" y="914400"/>    <!-- X and Y offsets -->
  <a:ext cx="6096000" cy="1828800"/> <!-- Width and height -->
</a:xfrm>

These are EMUs (English Metric Units). OOXML did not invent the unit. Office's older binary drawing format already measured in EMUs, and OOXML kept it.

The number was chosen so that the units people actually design in all come out as whole numbers:

  • 1 inch = 914,400 EMUs
  • 1 cm = 360,000 EMUs
  • 1 mm = 36,000 EMUs
  • 1 point = 12,700 EMUs
  • 1 pixel at 96 DPI = 9,525 EMUs

One EMU is about 28 nanometres. Because an inch, a centimetre, a point, and a pixel are each a whole number of EMUs, a layout can be stored in integers and converted between units without rounding drift.

3. The Shape Tree (p:spTree)

Everything on a slide sits in its <p:spTree> (shape tree): 

<p:sld>
  <p:cSld>
    <p:spTree>
      <p:sp>...</p:sp>            <!-- Shape or text box -->
      <p:grpSp>...</p:grpSp>      <!-- Group of shapes -->
      <p:graphicFrame>...</p:graphicFrame> <!-- Table or chart -->
      <p:pic>...</p:pic>          <!-- Picture -->
    </p:spTree>
  </p:cSld>
</p:sld>

DrawingML: The Shared Graphics Language

In Word, text is WordprocessingML (w:p, w:r, w:t). In PowerPoint, text is DrawingML (a:p, a:r, a:t), held in a shape's text body: 

<p:sp>
  <p:spPr>
    <a:xfrm>
      <a:off x="457200" y="457200"/>
      <a:ext cx="8229600" cy="1143000"/>
    </a:xfrm>
    <a:prstGeom prst="rect"><a:avLst/></a:prstGeom>
  </p:spPr>
  <p:txBody>
    <a:bodyPr anchor="t"/>
    <a:p>
      <a:r>
        <a:rPr sz="2800" b="1"/> <!-- 28pt bold -->
        <a:t>Slide Title Text</a:t>
      </a:r>
    </a:p>
  </p:txBody>
</p:sp>

Font size (sz) is in hundredths of a point, so 2800 is 28pt. A table lives in a <p:graphicFrame> as an <a:tbl>, and each of its cells has its own text body.

4. Reconstructing Reading Order from a 2D Canvas

This is the hardest problem in reading presentations: reading order.

If a slide has a title, a left column, and a right column, in what order do they appear in the XML?

The order of the shape tree is the stacking order, back to front. That usually follows the order in which the shapes were created, changed by any "Send to Back" or "Bring to Front". A title that was deleted and added again late can easily be the last element in <p:spTree>.

There are three common strategies, and none is free:

Strategy How it works Where it fails
Document order Read shapes in the order the XML lists them. A title or caption can come out after the text it belongs with.
Placeholder priority Put type="title" first, then type="body". Does nothing for plain text boxes, which are not placeholders.
Spatial sorting Group shapes into horizontal bands by their Y position, then sort each band left to right. Overlapping shapes, diagrams, and columns of unequal height have no single right answer.

5. Speaker Notes: The Detached Stream

Often the most useful text in a presentation is not on the slide at all. It is in the speaker notes.

PowerPoint does not store notes inside the slide. Instead, ppt/slides/_rels/slide1.xml.rels holds a relationship pointing to ../notesSlides/notesSlide1.xml.

A notes slide is a small slide of its own. It has one shape that shows a thumbnail of the slide it belongs to, and another (a placeholder of type="body") holding the notes text.

How officeParser Reads a Presentation

Here is what the library does as of version 8.1.1, and what it does not.

  1. Each slide becomes a slide node, in the order the presentation's slide list shows them. A slide's slideNumber is its place in the deck, not the number in its file name.
  2. A title placeholder (title or ctrTitle) becomes a level-1 heading. Other text becomes paragraphs and lists.
  3. Tables, charts (with their data), pictures, SmartArt text, and comments are read.
  4. Speaker notes are attached to the slide that names them, as slide.notes. Set ignoreNotes: true to leave them out.
  5. Slide masters are kept apart in ast.auxiliary.slideMasters, so their boilerplate does not mix into the slides.
import { parseOffice } from 'officeparser';

const ast = await parseOffice('pitch-deck.pptx');

for (const slide of ast.content) {
    const title = slide.children?.find(n => n.type === 'heading');
    console.log(title?.text, '| notes:', slide.notes?.length ?? 0);
}

const { value: markdown } = await ast.to('markdown');

In the Markdown, slides are separated by ---, the title is a # heading, and each speaker note is a block quote that starts with > **Note:**.

Two honest limits

Reading order is document order. officeParser reads shapes in the order the file lists them and marks titles as headings. It does not re-sort shapes by position, so a slide whose shape tree is out of visual order comes out in that order.

Inherited formatting is not resolved. Formatting comes from the slide's own XML. A font size or colour that a slide inherits from its layout, master, or theme is not filled in.
